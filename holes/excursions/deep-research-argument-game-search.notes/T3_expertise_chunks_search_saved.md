# T3: Expertise as pattern recognition vs. search, and how much search a learned prior saves (claim verification)

Verdict legend: CONFIRMED / CORRECTED / PARTLY CONFIRMED / UNSUPPORTED (could not verify against a primary source).

## Claim 1: de Groot (1946/1965): masters do not search deeper or more nodes; they consider better moves first

### Takeaway
PARTLY CONFIRMED. de Groot reported no gross skill differences in the macrostructure of search, including depth and number of moves considered; the advantage was in which moves came to mind. A 2011 replication largely agrees but finds a small significant difference in mean depth. The edition is usually cited as 1946 Dutch / 1965 English (1978 2nd ed.). Gobet & Simon cite it as "De Groot (1946/1978)".

### Cited Findings
- Chase & Simon (as summarised in the de Groot literature) report that de Groot found "no gross differences" between masters and weaker players in the number of moves considered, search heuristics, or depth of search. — [Connors, Burns & Campitelli 2011, Cognitive Science (search summary)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2011.01196.x); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51701426_Expertise_in_Complex_Decision_Making_The_Role_of_Search_in_Chess_70_Years_After_de_Groot)
- Connors et al. (2011) replicated de Groot's think-aloud task. Masters and intermediates showed no clear difference in depth of search, only a non-significant trend toward greater *maximum* depth for masters. The authors say this "support[s] de Groot's original conclusions". However, in a combined analysis the same paper reports a statistically significant difference in *mean* depth. — [Connors et al. 2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2011.01196.x)
- Gobet & Simon (1998) note that de Groot "emphasised that his Grandmasters and Masters were able to integrate rapidly the different parts of the positions ... into a whole — something weaker players could not do". They also restate the Chase–Simon reading that "chess Masters can generally choose good moves with only moderate look-ahead". — [Gobet & Simon 1998, Memory 6(3):225–255, pp. 225–227](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet%20&%20Simon-1998-Memory.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Masters don't search deeper" is fair as a summary of de Groot's own small-sample data (he studied a handful of grandmasters, masters, experts and class players). It should not be quoted as a law, because later studies with harder positions find depth differences (see Claim 5).
- "They consider better candidate moves first" matches the standard reading: the best move appeared among the masters' early candidate moves. I did not retrieve the page in de Groot that says this.

### Gaps
- I did not access de Groot's book directly. The often-quoted figures (for example, average maximum depth of about 6.8 vs 6.6 plies for GMs vs experts, and a similar number of base moves) come from memory and are **not verified** here. Do not quote them without checking *Thought and Choice in Chess* (1965/1978), ch. 4 / Table 9 area.

## Claim 2: Chase & Simon 1973: masters recall real positions far better, random positions hardly better

### Takeaway
CONFIRMED, with a correction. Chase & Simon (1973a) reported no skill effect for random positions. Later meta-analytic work by Gobet & Simon shows a *small but consistent* skill advantage on random positions. "Hardly better" is the accurate phrasing; "no better" is the overstatement.

### Cited Findings
- Gobet & Simon (1998) write: "contrary to Chase and Simon (1973a), the recall of random chess positions varied somewhat with skill in our experiment, although the effect is not statistically significant". They add: "We show elsewhere (Gobet & Simon, 1996c) that a small effect of skill on recall in random positions is observed consistently in other studies." — [Gobet & Simon 1998, p. 237–238](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet%20&%20Simon-1998-Memory.pdf)
- Their own numbers (5-s presentation):

  | Position type | Masters | Experts | Class A |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Game positions (% pieces recalled) | 92.0% | 57.1% | 32.2% |
  | Random positions (% pieces recalled) | 19.0% | 13.8% | 12.4% |

  Masters recalled "nearly three times as many pieces as Class A players in game positions, but only 1½ times as many in random positions". — [Gobet & Simon 1998, p. 237](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet%20&%20Simon-1998-Memory.pdf)
- The 1996c paper referenced is Gobet & Simon, "Recall of random and distorted chess positions: Implications for the theory of expertise" (Memory & Cognition 1996). — [ResearchGate entry](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225336454_Recall_of_random_and_distorted_chess_positions_Implications_for_the_theory_of_expertise)

### Inferences
- The memory advantage is domain-structured: it shrinks to a small residual on random boards. This is the classic evidence that expertise lives in stored patterns, not in general memory capacity.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Chase & Simon 1973 (Cognitive Psychology 4:55–81) itself. The often-quoted first-trial figures (master about 16 pieces vs novice about 4, from a single master, a class A player and a beginner) are **from memory, not verified**.

## Claim 3: Number of chunks a master holds (50,000? 300,000?)

### Takeaway
CONFIRMED with corrections. Simon & Gilmartin (1973) estimated a *range* of 10,000–100,000 chunks; "50,000" is the conventional midpoint. Gobet & Simon later raised the estimate to about 300,000. The sources disagree on which Gobet paper first gives 300,000.

### Cited Findings
- "Simon and Gilmartin estimated that expertise in chess would require between 10,000 and 100,000 chunks in memory (in the literature, 50,000 is often mentioned)." The estimate comes from their MAPP simulation program. — [Gobet & Simon 1998, p. 227](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet%20&%20Simon-1998-Memory.pdf)
- Holding (1985) argued that the roughly 50,000 estimate was "much too large". Gobet & Simon (1998) replied that "recent data (Gobet & Simon, 1996b; Saariluoma, 1994) support this estimate". So as of 1998 Gobet & Simon *defended* about 50,000. — [Gobet & Simon 1998, p. 228](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet%20&%20Simon-1998-Memory.pdf)
- PLOS ONE (Chassy & Gobet 2011) states that Gobet and Simon (2000, "Five seconds or sixty? Presentation time in expert memory", Cognitive Science 24) proposed "as many as 300,000 chunks". It notes that the estimate is indirect, coming from computer simulations (CHREST) of the recall task. — [PLOS ONE 2011](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0026692)
- **Disagreement on the source of 300,000:** A thesis (Cook) attributes the 300,000 estimate to Gobet & Simon 1996. It gives a gradient of about 500 chunks (class A), 10,000 (expert) and 300,000 (master). — [Cook, "Computational Chunking in Chess" (thesis, CORE)](https://core.ac.uk/works/345132) (search snippet; not read in full)

### Inferences
- Cite it as "Simon & Gilmartin 1973: 10,000–100,000 (≈50,000); Gobet & Simon 2000 (CHREST simulations): ≈300,000". The safest wording for the 300,000 figure attributes it to Gobet & Simon 2000, Cognitive Science, as PLOS ONE does. Note that it is a model-derived estimate, not a measurement.

### Gaps
- I did not open Gobet & Simon 2000 to get the page of the 300,000 figure.

## Claim 4: Template theory (Gobet & Simon 1996), CHREST, and Klein's RPD (1989/1998)

### Takeaway
CONFIRMED in substance. Template theory (Gobet & Simon 1996) extends chunking with large "templates", that is, chunks with slots, linked to plans and moves, and is implemented in CHREST. Gobet's SEARCH model (1997) applies it to look-ahead. For Klein's RPD, the canonical sources are Klein, Calderwood & Clinton-Cirocco 1986 (fireground) and *Sources of Power* (1998). The "1989" date is a real secondary citation but not the origin.

### Cited Findings
- Template theory: Gobet & Simon "extend the chunking theory to take account of the evidence for larger retrieval structures (templates) in long-term memory". Templates have slots and hold "pointers to symbols representing plans, moves, strategical and tactical concepts, as well as other templates". — [Gobet & Simon 1998, abstract & p. 228–229](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet%20&%20Simon-1998-Memory.pdf)
- Primary template-theory paper: Gobet & Simon (1996), "Templates in chess memory: A mechanism for recalling several boards", Cognitive Psychology 31:1–40. Bibliographic entry: [Gobet bibliography, chrest.info](http://chrest.info/fg/bibliography-by-topic.html). CHREST model page: [chrest.info](http://chrest.info/fg/papers/ModelChessMem/Chess%20Memory.html).
- The chunking theory's link to move choice: "chunks, upon recognition, would suggest good moves to the Masters, which could then be further evaluated by limited look-ahead search." — [Gobet & Simon 1998, p. 227](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet%20&%20Simon-1998-Memory.pdf)
- Gobet (1997), "A pattern-recognition theory of search in expert problem solving", Thinking & Reasoning 3:291–313, introduces the SEARCH model. Templates aid search through faster LTM storage and search in a "template space" alongside move space. The model accounts for the *small* skill difference in average search depth. — [PhilArchive record](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/GOBAPT); [Brunel repository](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/843); [chessprogramming wiki](https://chessprogramming.org/CHUMP)
- RPD: Klein's model of fast decisions in complex settings, developed on fireground commanders. — [Wikipedia: Recognition-primed decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recognition-primed_decision)
- Commanders made about 80% of their decisions in under a minute (Sources of Power, p. 4, as quoted). — [Fire Engineering](https://www.fireengineering.com/leadership/fireground-management/fireground-command-decisions/)
- First fireground paper: Klein, Calderwood & Clinton-Cirocco (1986). The model drew little attention until *Sources of Power* (1998). — [Psychology Today, Klein blog](https://www.psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/seeing-what-others-dont/202102/the-rpd-model-criticisms-and-confusions)

### Inferences
- RPD's structure is "recognise a typical case, take the first workable option, mentally simulate to check it". It is the field analogue of "policy proposes, limited search verifies".

### Gaps
- I could not confirm a 1989 primary RPD paper. Klein 1989 ("Recognition-primed decisions", Advances in Man-Machine Systems Research 5:47–92) is the likely referent, but I did not verify it. Cite 1986 and 1998 instead.

## Claim 5: Experts DO search somewhat more (Charness 1981; Gobet; Campitelli & Gobet 2004)

### Takeaway
CONFIRMED. "Experts search less, not deeper" is an oversimplification. de Groot found no difference. Charness 1981 found modest depth gains with skill. Campitelli & Gobet 2004 found large skill effects in depth and node counts on very complex positions. Gobet's synthesis: experts adapt, using recognition on simple positions or under time pressure and extensive search on complex positions when time allows. The two literatures disagree on magnitude; this should be flagged.

### Cited Findings
- Charness (1981), "Search in chess: Age and skill differences", J. Exp. Psychol.: HPP 7(2):467–476. Secondary summaries report that stronger players search somewhat deeper. — [PLOS ONE 2011 (lit. review)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0026692); [Chessable summary](https://www.chessable.com/blog/cognitive-skill-studies-and-chess/)
- Campitelli & Gobet (2004), "Adaptive expert decision making: Skilled chessplayers search more and deeper", ICGA Journal 27:209–216. Using very complex positions, they found a strong skill effect on depth of search and number of nodes generated. The differences were sometimes about 10× larger than in previous research. The authors propose that experts switch between pattern recognition (simple positions or time pressure) and extensive search (complex positions with time). — [PLOS ONE 2011 summary of Campitelli & Gobet](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0026692)
- Connors et al. (2011) found a significant mean-depth difference in a combined analysis, despite no clear difference in the main replication. — [Connors et al. 2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2011.01196.x)
- Gobet's SEARCH model is designed to explain a "small skill difference in average search depth". — [PhilArchive](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/GOBAPT)
- Related: Burns (2004) and Moxley et al. studied fast vs slow processes under time pressure. — [Psychological Research 2006 (time pressure study)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-006-0076-0)

### Inferences
- The defensible wording: experts' advantage is *mainly* in candidate selection, which is recognition. Given hard positions and time, they *also* search more and deeper. Search and recognition are complementary, not alternatives.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Charness 1981 directly. The commonly repeated claim that depth increases with rating up to about 2100 Elo and then plateaus is **from memory, unverified**.
- One search summary said "grandmasters calculate variations slightly deeper, but the same number of variations as candidate masters". The underlying study for this line was unclear, so I did not use it.

## Claim 7: Anthony, Tian & Barber 2017, Expert Iteration (ExIt): dual-process framing and search saved

### Takeaway
CONFIRMED. The paper explicitly frames itself in Kahneman-style dual-process terms: the network is System 1, tree search is System 2. It does not report a "nodes saved" metric as such. It does report search-equivalence comparisons: a policy-guided MCTS beats vanilla MCTS 97% at the same iteration count, while doubling vanilla iterations gives only 56%. ExIt with 10k iterations beats MoHex with 10× more iterations.

### Cited Findings
- Framing (Introduction): "System 1 is a fast, unconscious and automatic mode of thought, also known as intuition or heuristic process. System 2 ... is a slow, conscious, explicit ...". It continues: "strong intuitions allow for more effective analytic reasoning by rapidly selecting interesting lines of play ... Repeated deep study gradually improves intuitions." Deep RL policy networks with no lookahead are "analogous to System 1"; ExIt "uses a Tree Search as an analogue of System 2". — [Anthony, Tian & Barber 2017, arXiv:1705.08439, §1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)
- Apprentice/expert: the imitation-learning step "is analogous to a human improving their intuition for the task by studying" the expert's analysis. Apprentice nets give fast evaluations and can be corrected by lookahead, "just as human players can correct inaccurate intuitions through lookahead". — [arXiv:1705.08439, §3, §5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)
- Search-equivalence result (§5.1): N-MCTS with the strongest TPT policy net "significantly outperforms our baseline MCTS, winning 97% of games". The network "cause[s] a two times slowdown in search. For comparison, a doubling of the number of iterations of the vanilla MCTS results in a win rate of 56%." — [arXiv:1705.08439, §5.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)
- vs MoHex 1.0 (Hex Olympiad champion): ExIt with 10,000 iterations won the following shares of games:

  | MoHex setting | ExIt win rate |
  |---|---|
  | 10,000 iterations | 75.3% |
  | 100,000 iterations | 59.3% |
  | 4 s/move (parallel solver on; over 6× slower than ExIt's searcher) | 55.6% |

  — [arXiv:1705.08439, §6](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)
- The paper notes that AlphaGo Zero independently developed a version of ExIt. — [arXiv:1705.08439, §7](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)

### Inferences
- ExIt measures the prior's value as *win-rate at a fixed search budget* and as *beating opponents given 10× the iterations*. This can be read as "the prior is worth more than 10× search" against MoHex (which has its own priors and solver). It is not a direct count of nodes saved.

### Gaps
- There is no explicit "nodes-to-solve" curve in ExIt.

## Claim 8: Literature that measures nodes expanded or search steps as the metric of what a learned prior saves

### Takeaway
CONFIRMED that such literature exists. The clearest examples:
- Searchformer (Lehnert et al. 2024) explicitly counts search steps vs A*.
- LevinTS (Orseau, Lelis, Lattimore, Weber 2018) proves node-expansion bounds as a function of the policy's probability on the solution.

Stream of Search (Gandhi et al. 2024) measures accuracy, not search steps.

### Cited Findings
- **Searchformer** (Lehnert, Sukhbaatar, Su, Zheng, McVay, Rabbat, Tian 2024, arXiv:2402.14083): a Transformer that "optimally solves previously unseen Sokoban puzzles 93.7% of the time, while using up to 26.8% fewer search steps than the A* implementation that was used for training". It "outperforms baselines that predict the optimal plan directly with a 5-10× smaller model size". — [arXiv:2402.14083](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14083)
- **Stream of Search** (Gandhi et al. 2024, arXiv:2404.03683): represents search "as a flattened string — a stream of search (SoS)" on Countdown.
  - "SoS pretraining increases search accuracy by 25% over models trained to predict only the optimal search trajectory".
  - Finetuned (STaR/APA) models "solve 36% of previously unsolved problems".
  - The metric is solve rate/accuracy, **not** nodes expanded. — [arXiv:2404.03683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03683)
- **LevinTS / LubyTS** (Orseau et al., NeurIPS 2018, "Single-Agent Policy Tree Search With Guarantees", arXiv:1811.10928):
  - "we can bound the number of node expansions — roughly speaking, the search time — depending on the probability of the sequences of actions that reach the goal".
  - LevinTS expands nodes in order of d(n)/π(n) and has a strict upper bound on nodes searched before the least-cost solution.
  - Validated on 1,000 Sokoban levels with an A3C-trained policy, competitive with a state-of-the-art domain-independent planner. — [arXiv:1811.10928, abstract & §1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10928)

### Inferences
- LevinTS gives the cleanest formal statement of "how much a prior saves": expansions scale roughly as depth / π(solution). A better prior means higher π, which means fewer nodes. This is the theoretical counterpart of "the chunk names the subtree".
- For classical planning there is a large literature on learned heuristics (e.g., hypergraph/graph networks for heuristics). It conventionally reports node expansions and coverage. I did not verify specific papers in this pass.

### Gaps
- Neural-guided SAT/SMT (NeuroSAT, NeuroCore, Selsam & Bjørner 2019) and learned planning heuristics (e.g., Shen, Trevizan & Thiébaux 2020 STRIPS-HGN) were not fetched. Their exact metrics (problems solved vs decisions/conflicts vs expansions) are **unverified** here.
- "Learning to search" in combinatorial optimisation was not covered.

## Claim 9: Kahneman System 1/2 applied to AI (Booch et al. 2021), and "name a subtree as a known chunk, hand the rest to an exact evaluator"

### Takeaway
Booch et al.: CONFIRMED, but the venue/year should read "AAAI 2021; arXiv Oct 2020" (arXiv:2010.06002). For the specific "name a subtree as a known chunk with known value, hand the rest to an exact evaluator" formulation, the claim is UNSUPPORTED: I found no source stating it in that form. The closest documented ideas are:
- Chase & Simon / Gobet: chunks suggest moves, which limited look-ahead then evaluates.
- Gobet 1997: search in "template space".
- ExIt: the intuition proposes, search corrects.
- LevinTS: policy probability bounds expansions.

### Cited Findings
- Booch, Fabiano, Horesh, Kate, Lenchner, Linck, Loreggia, Murugesan, Mattei, Rossi, Srivastava, "Thinking Fast and Slow in AI" (IBM et al.; arXiv:2010.06002, v2 Dec 2020; AAAI 2021 senior-member/blue-sky track). It is a research-direction paper: it "draws inspiration from cognitive theories of human decision making" (Kahneman's System 1/System 2) to obtain capabilities such as adaptability, generalisability, common sense and causal reasoning. It proposes research questions rather than empirical results. — [arXiv:2010.06002](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06002)
- ExIt (2017) already used the System 1/2 framing for network + tree search, so it predates Booch et al. on this framing in game AI. — [arXiv:1705.08439 §1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)
- Closest psychological statement of "chunk → value/move → hand to search": "chunks, upon recognition, would suggest good moves to the Masters, which could then be further evaluated by limited look-ahead search". Templates also carry pointers to "plans, moves, strategical and tactical concepts". — [Gobet & Simon 1998, pp. 227–229](http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/II%20Donnees%20empiriques/Gobet%20&%20Simon-1998-Memory.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the literature, recognition supplies *candidates and evaluations* (policy/value) and search *verifies*. The proposed framing ("chunk = subtree with known value; exact evaluator for the residual") is closer to a different classical architecture:
  - endgame tablebases or transposition tables inside alpha-beta, where a recognised subposition is replaced by its exact, stored value;
  - macro-operators/options in planning.
- It should be presented as a synthesis or novel claim, not as attributed prior work, unless a specific source turns up.

### Gaps
- I did not find a paper that explicitly argues the pattern step should "name a subtree as a known chunk with a known value and hand the rest to an exact evaluator". The search was limited (about 20 tool calls); a targeted search on tablebase-in-search, "knowledge-based search pruning" (e.g., Berliner's work, or Wilkins' PARADISE chess program, which used pattern knowledge to guide small searches) might find antecedents.
